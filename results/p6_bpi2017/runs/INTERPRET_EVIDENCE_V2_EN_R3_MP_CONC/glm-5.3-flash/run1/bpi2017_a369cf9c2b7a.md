### Observed differences

- Resource allocation changed across six activities. In the earlier window, assignments for A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A22 were led by one group of resources — for example User_51 held about 7–13% of the assignments of these activities — while roughly 40–50% of each activity's assignments were spread over a long tail of many others. In the later window, a largely different group leads — for example User_24 takes about 11% of A22's assignments and User_28 about 7–9% on several activities — and the earlier leaders no longer appear among the top ten, while the long-tail share stays similar (about 38–48%). [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]

- The redistribution is consistent in direction across all six activities: the same resources gain share everywhere and the same resources lose share everywhere. The profile dissimilarity is 0.39–0.53 (largest for A22), and each difference exceeds all but about 1 in 1000 random re-splits of the pooled data. The profiles rest on roughly 120–300 observations per window (A01 appears in essentially all 200 traces in both windows), and the resource family produced 6 qualifying items where about 0.005 would be expected if nothing had changed. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]

- One control-flow difference: in the earlier window, about 53% of A18's observed outgoing transitions led to A22 and about 33% to A10 (about 7% each to A03 and A12); in the later window, about 69% led to A10, about 12–13% each to A02 and A16, and only about 6% to A22. This rests on only about 15–16 observed outgoing transitions per window, and it is the single control-flow item against about 0.17 expected by chance — within the range a stable process typically produces. [DIR001]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the possibility of a change in resource allocation: across the activities A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A22, assignment work moved from one group of resources to a largely different group — for example, User_51, the most-used resource on several of these activities before (about 7–13% of assignments), drops out of the leading positions, while resources such as User_24 and User_28 take over (about 6–11%). The activities themselves still occur at similar rates in both windows, so the change lies in who is assigned the work, not in whether the activities run. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]

Supporting signs: all six assignment profiles shifted in the same direction with the same rising and falling resources; every difference is larger than in about 999.5 of 1000 random re-splits; and the resource family yielded 6 items against about 0.005 expected without change, many times more than a stable process produces, so chance cannot explain the pattern as a whole. Limitations: A13 and A14 show identical profiles, so the six items are not fully independent confirmations; and the evidence shows only who received the work — it cannot rule out that a changed mix of cases was routed to different resources rather than the same work being reassigned. [ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN006]

Other differences: the outgoing transitions of A18 shifted from mostly A22 (about 53%) to mostly A10 (about 69%), with A02 and A16 appearing at about 12–13% each; with only about 15–16 observed transitions per window and a single control-flow item against about 0.17 expected by chance, this does not by itself support a structural change and is not part of the main hypothesis.
