### Observed differences

- Resource allocation changed across six activities. In the earlier window, assignments for A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A22 were led by one group of resource codes — User_51 held the largest single share on A01, A03 and A22 (roughly 8–13 in 100 assignments), with no code above about 8 in 100 on the other activities — while in the later window a largely different group leads: User_24 is largest on A22 (about 11 in 100) and User_76 largest on A13, A14 and A17 (about 8–9 in 100), and the earlier leading codes fall out of the top shares. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]
- The redistribution is consistent in direction across all six activities: the same codes lose share and a largely disjoint set gains it, with profile dissimilarity of about 0.39–0.53 on a 0–1 scale, each larger than in all but about 1 in 2000 random re-splits. The share of assignments outside the top codes stays similar (roughly 38–50 in 100 in both windows), so the work remains spread over many codes and only the leading codes change. The profiles rest on roughly 120–300 assignment observations per activity per window, and participation is stable (A01 occurs in all 200 traces in both windows, A03 in about 199 in 200). - The only control-flow difference concerns A18: in the earlier window about 53 in 100 of its observed outgoing transitions went to A22 and about 33 in 100 to A10, while in the later window about 69 in 100 went to A10, A22 dropped to about 6 in 100, and A02 and A16 each appeared at about 12–13 in 100. This rests on only 15 versus 16 observed transitions. [DIR001]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

A change in resource allocation is supported: in the later window, work on A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A22 was handled by a largely different group of resource codes than in the earlier window — the previously leading codes, including User_51, lost their top shares, and a largely disjoint group, including User_24 and User_76, took over — while how often the activities themselves run stayed stable. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]

Supporting signs: the resource family produced six qualifying items from 10 tested units, where about 0.005 would be expected if nothing had changed — a count many times that expectation, so the pattern as a whole is not what random re-splits produce; the six items are direct allocation measures that move coherently, with overlapping rising and falling code groups; and stable participation counts rule out that the shift merely reflects the activities running more or less often. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]

Limitations: the codes are anonymous and the profiles show only who handled work in each window, not how assignment was decided, so the evidence cannot distinguish a change in assignment behaviour from a period-bound turnover in which codes were active; also, A13 and A14 show identical profiles, so those two items likely reflect the same underlying assignments rather than two independent confirmations. [ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004]

Other differences: the redistribution of A18's outgoing transitions (A22 losing roughly half its share to A10, with A02 and A16 appearing) is the single control-flow item, against about 0.17 expected from 35 tested units — within the range an unchanged process produces — and it rests on 15–16 observations, so it may be a sampling fluctuation or a rare path and is not part of the main hypothesis.
