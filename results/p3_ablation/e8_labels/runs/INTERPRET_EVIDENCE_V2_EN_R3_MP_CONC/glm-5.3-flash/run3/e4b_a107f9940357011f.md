### Observed differences

- Cancellations became concentrated on a single resource: in the earlier window, the resource carrying out A12 was spread over about 44 distinct resources, with the largest single one handling about 40 in 100 of the cases (concentration index about 0.18); in the later window, one resource (User_36) handled all observed cancellations (share 100 in 100, concentration index 1.0). The share of traces containing A12 stayed similar (199 of 200 before, 189 of 200 after), so this is not a small-sample effect. [ASSIGN003, CONC001]
- Offer creation and sending were reassigned: for A13 and A14, User_76 fell from about 11 in 100 assignments in the earlier window to not being observed, while User_71 rose from about 6 in 100 to about 24 in 100; for O_Sent the same shift appears (User_71 about 6 to about 25 in 100; User_76, previously about 13 in 100, no longer observed). [ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]
- Acceptance and completion work changed hands as well: for A01, A03 and A22, several resources that each held roughly 3–12 in 100 of the assignments in the earlier window no longer appear, and a largely different set of resources takes over at similar shares, while the pooled remainder stays around 38–48 in 100 in both windows. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN007]
- The elapsed time between consecutive completions of A03 and A13 shortened markedly: the median fell from about 168 hours to about 66 hours (mean about 167 to 72 hours), the middle half of intervals narrowed from roughly 115–195 hours to roughly 48–77 hours, and more such intervals were observed (58 before, 80 after); the difference exceeds about 9995 of 10000 random re-splits. [TWAIT001]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the possibility that resource allocation changed in the later window. Cancellations (A12), previously shared across a wide pool of about 44 resources, became the exclusive work of a single resource (User_36), and offer creation and sending (A13 / A14, O_Sent) shifted from User_76, previously the largest single contributor at about 11–13 in 100, to User_71, whose share roughly quadrupled to about 24–25 in 100; assignment mixes for A01, A03 and A22 similarly passed from one group of resources to a largely different one. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007, CONC001]

The screening comparison supports this: eight resource items qualified where about 0.005 would be expected by chance for that family (one time item against about 0.02; no control-flow items against about 0.18), the resource measures capture allocation directly, and each item rests on 121–343 supporting observations. Limitations: items describing the same underlying change are not independent confirmations (ASSIGN003 with CONC001; ASSIGN004 with ASSIGN005), and the evidence cannot distinguish a change in assignment behaviour from a change in which resources were active in each window, since the resource codes are anonymous. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007, CONC001]

Other differences: The elapsed time between consecutive completions of A03 and A13 was much shorter in the later window (median about 66 hours versus about 168 hours) [TWAIT001]; as the only time item — about the rate an unchanged process produces (about 0.02 expected for that family) — it does not by itself establish a timing change.
