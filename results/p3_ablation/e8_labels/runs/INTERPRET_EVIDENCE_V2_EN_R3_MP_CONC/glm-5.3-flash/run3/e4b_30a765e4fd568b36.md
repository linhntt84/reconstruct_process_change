### Observed differences

- In the earlier window, assignment work at all four profiled activities was spread over a long tail of resources: resources outside the ten most used handled about 56 in 100 assignments of A03, about 54 in 100 for A13/A14, and about 45 in 100 for A22. In the later window these shares fell to about 48 in 100 for A03 and the offer activities, and to about 28 in 100 for A22, so work is now concentrated on fewer resources. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004]
- The most-used resources changed as a group. In the later window, codes that previously held only small shares now lead: User_67 takes about 7 in 100 of A03 assignments and about 9 in 100 of offer assignments without being among the top shares before, and User_79 rises from about 5 to 13 in 100 for A22. Codes that led before, such as User_65 with about 5–8 in 100 across the activities, no longer appear among the top shares. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004]
- Participation in the activities themselves was broadly stable: the observation bases behind the profiles are similar in both windows (A03 200 → 198, the offer activities 277 → 270, A22 119 → 100 observations). [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004]
- The elapsed time between consecutive completions of A22 and A03 was redistributed: the median gap stayed at about 10 milliseconds, but the spread roughly tripled (standard deviation from about 2.6 to 8.1 milliseconds) and the 75th percentile rose from about 11 to 13 milliseconds, while the number of such intervals fell from 119 to 99. ### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports a change in resource allocation: in the later window, the work for A03, A13/A14 and A22 is reassigned from a broad set of lightly used resources to a smaller, more concentrated set, with a group of previously minor codes — including User_67 and User_79 — taking the largest shares and a group of previously leading codes — including User_65 — falling to negligible shares. This rests on four assignment profiles that move in the same direction with overlapping groups of rising and falling codes, on a resource family that produced 4 evidence items where about 0.005 would be expected if nothing had changed (far beyond what chance produces), and on differences larger than in about 1999 of 2000 random re-splits, with substantial observation bases in both windows. Limitations: the two offer-related profiles are numerically identical and likely describe the same underlying events, so they are not independent confirmations; and the evidence shows only assignment shares, so a change in which resources are active at all cannot be distinguished from a change in how work is distributed among the same resources. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004]

Other differences: the single timing item shows the redistribution of completion-to-completion gaps described above, but the time family produced 1 item against about 0.02 expected without change, which is what a stable process typically produces, so it does not by itself support a timing change; the fall in A22 observations (119 → 100, roughly 60 to 50 in 100 traces) did not qualify as a control-flow item (0 items against about 0.17 expected) and could be sampling variation. [TWAIT001, ASSIGN004]
