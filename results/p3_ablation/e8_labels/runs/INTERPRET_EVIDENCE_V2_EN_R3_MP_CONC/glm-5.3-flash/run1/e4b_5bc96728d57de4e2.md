### Observed differences

- On every activity with allocation data, work moved between groups of resources. In the earlier window, A22 was dominated by User_56 (about 20 in 100 assignments) and User_78 (about 16 in 100); in the later window User_56 fell to about 12 in 100 and User_78 no longer appears among the ten most frequent resources (near 0 in 100), while User_42 rose to about 9 in 100 from under 3 in 100 [ASSIGN005].
- The same direction of change holds on all five activities: one set of resources (including User_78 and User_56) loses share everywhere, and another set (including User_42, about 5 to about 10 in 100 on A01) gains everywhere. The shift is largest for A22 (dissimilarity 0.43 versus about 0.27–0.29 for the others), where work going to resources outside the ten most frequent grew from about 20 to about 33 in 100 [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005].
- The activities themselves still occur at similar rates (A03 in 199 vs 197 supporting traces, A22 96 vs 102, A13 251 vs 259), so the difference lies in which resources performed the work, not in whether it was performed [ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN005].
- In the later window, the elapsed time between the completions of A04 and A01 is much shorter: median about 68 hours down to about 21 hours, mean about 57 to about 29 hours, in all 200 traces in both windows; the spread widened (standard deviation about 47 to 76 hours), so times became shorter on average but more variable [TWAIT002].
- The elapsed time between the completions of A09 and A23 also shortened, median from about 13 hours to about 1 hour; this pair appears in 35 vs 25 of 200 traces [TWAIT004].
- Two further flagged gaps (A12–A02 and A05–A09) last a small fraction of a second in both windows; they are statistically flagged but practically negligible [TWAIT001, TWAIT003].

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the possibility that resource allocation changed: across A01, A03, A13/A14 and A22, work shifted from a group of previously dominant resources to a different, broader group of resources. This is measured directly on each activity's assignment distribution, all five profiles move consistently in the same direction with the same resources losing and gaining, and each difference exceeds about 999.5 in 1,000 random re-splits of the pooled data [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005].

The screening comparison supports this at the family level: the resource family flagged 5 items against roughly 0.005 expected without change and the time family 4 against about 0.015, while control flow produced none (0 against about 0.16 expected), so the flagged items as a whole are unlikely if nothing had changed, and no structural change is indicated [TWAIT001, TWAIT002, TWAIT003, TWAIT004, ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005].

Limitations: the evidence cannot distinguish a targeted handover from the losing resources to the gaining ones from a general dispersal of work across more resources — on A22 the outside-top-ten share grew to about 33 in 100 — and the anonymous codes permit no inference about roles or teams; whether the shortening of elapsed times is connected to the reallocation cannot be determined from this evidence [ASSIGN005, TWAIT002, TWAIT004].

Other differences: a timing change in its own right — in the later window, elapsed time between A04 and A01 completions and between A09 and A23 completions shortened markedly (medians about 68 to 21 hours and about 13 to 1 hour), with the latter pair also occurring in fewer traces (35 vs 25 of 200); the two remaining flagged gaps are sub-second in both windows and practically negligible [TWAIT002, TWAIT004, TWAIT001, TWAIT003].
