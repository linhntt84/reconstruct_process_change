### Observed differences

- Resource allocation changed across six activities (A01, A03, A13, A14, A17, A22). In the earlier window the work was spread broadly: User_39 held the largest single share of each activity, at roughly 8–11 in 100 assignments, while User_46 held only about 4–6 in 100. In the later window User_46 is the largest contributor for every one of these activities, at about 15–19 in 100, while User_39 no longer appears among the top contributors at all. - The same redistribution pattern repeats in every profile: several resources that previously held roughly 3–6 in 100 of the assignments lost most of their share or dropped out of the top contributors, while a group of resources that were not top contributors before took on work — most visibly User_61, with up to about 9 in 100 of A22 assignments. The volume of observations behind each profile was similar in both windows (for example 273 vs 267 for the offer activities), so the shift is in shares, not in how often the activities ran. - A21 appeared in about 17.5 in 100 traces before (35 of 200) but only about 7.5 in 100 after (15 of 200); mean occurrences per trace fell by the same amount, so this is participation, not repetition. The later figure rests on only 15 traces. [OCC001]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

Main hypothesis: a change in resource allocation. In the earlier window, assignments for A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A22 were distributed across a broad set of resources led by User_39; in the later window the work is concentrated on User_46 (from about 4–6 in 100 to about 15–19 in 100 for each activity), User_39 and several other previously frequent resources no longer appear among the top contributors, and a group of newly prominent resources, including User_61, has taken over a comparable share. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]

Supporting signs: the shift points in the same direction in all six activity profiles, and each difference is larger than all but about 1 in 2000 random re-splits of the pooled data. The resource family produced 6 qualifying items where about 0.006 would be expected if nothing had changed, so chance cannot account for the family as a whole. Limitations: the identifiers are anonymous codes, so the evidence shows a shift between groups of codes, not who or why; and only the top contributors per activity are profiled, so smaller movements within the remaining roughly 43 in 100 "other" share are not visible. A competing explanation the evidence cannot rule out is turnover in which resources were active at all, rather than reallocation of the same work within a stable pool — both look identical in anonymous assignment shares. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]

Other differences: the drop in A21 participation (about 17.5 in 100 traces to about 7.5 in 100) is the single control-flow item, in a family where about 0.17 items were expected by chance and where the later window contributes only 15 traces; a stable process typically produces such an item, so it is not incorporated into the hypothesis.
