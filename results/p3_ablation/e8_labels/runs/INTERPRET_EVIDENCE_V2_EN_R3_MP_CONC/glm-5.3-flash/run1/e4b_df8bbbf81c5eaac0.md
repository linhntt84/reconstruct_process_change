### Observed differences

- In the later window, assignments of A01 were spread over more resource codes: codes outside the ten largest went from about 45 in 100 assignments to about 54 in 100, and the largest single code, User_19, fell from about 11 in 100 assignments to about 3–4 in 100, while several codes that were not among the largest before now hold roughly 3.5–5 in 100 each. [ASSIGN001]
- A03 shows the same pattern: the share outside the ten largest codes rose from about 45% to about 53%, User_19 fell from about 11 in 100 assignments to no longer being among the leading codes, and several codes newly appear among the largest shares at about 3.5–5 in 100 each. [ASSIGN002]
- For A07 the group of resources changed almost completely: earlier, ten codes covered about 82% of assignments (each roughly 6–11%) and only about 18% went to all remaining codes; in the later window only three of those ten are still among the leading shares, at lower levels (for example User_83, about 11 in 100 before, no longer appears), a largely new group leads — topped by User_126 at about 13 in 100 — and the remaining-codes share rose to about 31 in 100. [ASSIGN003]
- The activities themselves still occur at similar rates: A01 in essentially every trace in both windows, A03 in about 99 in 100, and A07 in about 74 in 100 of the earlier traces versus about 70 in 100 of the later ones — so the difference lies in who performed the work, not in whether the steps ran. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003]
- A24 was executed less in the later window: it appears in about 29 in 100 traces versus about 40 in 100 before, its mean occurrences per trace fell from about 0.6 to about 0.36, and traces with repeated executions fell from about 14% to about 6%. [OCC001]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports a specific possibility: in the later window, the allocation of work to resources changed for A01, A03 and A07, shifting from the groups of codes that previously dominated towards a broader and partly different set of codes — with User_19 losing most of its share on A01 and A03, and the group handling A07 being almost entirely replaced, now led by User_126. The supporting signs are direct and consistent: all three items are direct allocation measurements pointing in the same direction, the activities' occurrence rates are essentially unchanged (so the steps did not simply run more or less often), and the screening result cannot be explained by chance as a whole — the resource family produced three items where about 0.004 would be expected without a change, and each difference is larger than in more than 999 of 1,000 random re-splits. Limitations: the codes are anonymous, so the evidence shows a reallocation between groups of codes but not what drove it, and a shift in the mix of cases reaching these activities, which could reallocate work without any change in assignment behaviour, cannot be ruled out. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003]

Other differences: A24 occurred in fewer traces and less often per trace in the later window (about 29 in 100 traces versus about 40 in 100; mean occurrences about 0.36 versus about 0.6). This is the only control-flow item, against a family expectation of about 0.16 items without a change, so a single difference of this size — even one larger than about 995 of 1,000 random re-splits — is also what a stable process can produce by sampling; it is therefore not treated as an established change and is kept out of the main hypothesis. [OCC001]
