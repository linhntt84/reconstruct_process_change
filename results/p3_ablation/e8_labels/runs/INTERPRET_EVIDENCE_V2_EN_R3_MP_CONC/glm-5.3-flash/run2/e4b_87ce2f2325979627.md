### Observed differences

- Resource allocation became more concentrated. In the earlier window, each of the four offer-handling activities was spread thinly across many resources: no single resource held more than about 9 in 100 assignments, and resources outside the top ten together held about 47–55 in 100. In the later window, a small group dominated: User_3, previously outside the top ten, took about 11–13 in 100 assignments of every activity, User_39 and User_53 rose to roughly 5–9 in 100 each, and the pooled share of all remaining resources fell by about 10–13 points on every activity (for A22, from about 47 to about 35 in 100). - The same resources gained and lost across all four activities (A01, A03, A17, A22): the gainers and losers largely coincide, which points to one re-allocation of work rather than activity-specific shifts. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004]

- Each assignment profile differs far more between the windows than random re-splits of the pooled traces produce: every dissimilarity exceeds about 999.5 in 1000 reference comparisons. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004]

- Offer creation repeated less often. A13 and A14 still occur in every trace in both windows (participation 100% in both), but mean occurrences per trace fell from about 1.48 to about 1.26, and the share of traces in which the activity recurs fell from about 34 in 100 to about 20 in 100. The two items carry identical figures and describe the same trace behaviour, not two independent changes. [OCC001, OCC002]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the possibility that resource allocation changed: in the later window, the execution of A01, A03, A17 and A22 was concentrated on a small group of resources — User_3 above all, together with User_39 and User_53 — while a broad set of previously active resources lost roughly 2–7 percentage points per activity. Supporting signs: all four assignment profiles moved in the same direction with largely the same gainers and losers; the resource family produced 4 qualifying items where about 0.006 were expected if nothing had changed, so chance sampling cannot explain the family as a whole; and each item's difference exceeds about 999.5 in 1000 random re-splits. Limitations: the resource codes are anonymous and the profiles capture shares of assignment events, not workloads or roles, and the four profiles may reflect a single underlying re-allocation rather than four independent changes. A competing explanation the evidence cannot rule out is that the mix of cases shifted — which resources are involved per case — rather than the assignment behaviour itself.
