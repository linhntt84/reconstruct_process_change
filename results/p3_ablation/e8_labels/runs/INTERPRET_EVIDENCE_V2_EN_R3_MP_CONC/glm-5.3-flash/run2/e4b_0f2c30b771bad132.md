### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the hypothesis that resource allocation changed. In the later window, A11 work that had previously been shared across a pool of about 21 resources was handled entirely by the single resource User_123, and assignment of A03 and A21 concentrated on User_3, whose share rose from about 8 to about 19 in 100 and from about 11 to about 30 in 100 respectively, while several other resources lost shares. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, CONC001]

This rests on direct measurements of allocation and concentration. Across 80 tested units, five items qualified against about 0.19 expected without change; by family, control flow produced none (about 0.17 expected), time produced one (about 0.017 expected), and resources produced four against about 0.006 expected — a count far beyond what chance produces, and each difference was larger than in about 1,999 of 2,000 random re-splits of the pooled data. The two A11 items agree, but both describe the same traces, so they are one confirmation rather than two. [TWAIT001, ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, CONC001]

Limitations: resource codes are anonymous, so no roles, people or teams can be inferred, and the evidence shows the reallocation itself, not how it was brought about. An important competing explanation the evidence cannot rule out is a change in the mix of cases reaching these activities, which would also shift conditional assignment shares; with no control-flow items qualifying, the surrounding structure cannot be checked. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, CONC001]

Other differences: the elapsed time between the completions of A04 and A01 was much lower in the later window (median about 70 hours versus about 13 hours) [TWAIT001]. As the only time item, against about 0.017 expected without change, it is within what an unchanged process produces at these thresholds and cannot be separated from sampling variation; no structural evidence links it to the allocation change.
