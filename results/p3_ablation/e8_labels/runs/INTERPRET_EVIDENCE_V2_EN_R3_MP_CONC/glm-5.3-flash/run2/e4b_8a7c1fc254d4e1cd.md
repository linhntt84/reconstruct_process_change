### Observed differences

- Resource allocation changed wholesale across the offer-handling activities. In the earlier window, seven activities (A13 / A14, A17, A01, A03, A10, A22) were each dominated by a consistent set of resources holding individual shares of roughly 4–11 in 100 events; in the later window, most of these no longer hold top shares and a largely different set does. One resource that held about 7–11 in 100 of six of these activities (User_46) falls to at most about 5 in 100 or out of the top group, while User_39's share rises in all seven activities (for example from about 7 to about 13 in 100 on A22). In the earlier window, a small group of resources led by User_117 (about 17 in 100) covered about 86 in 100 A10 events; in the later window the largest single share is about 10 in 100, and resources outside the top ten cover about 52 in 100, up from about 14 in 100. - For the other six activities the overall degree of concentration barely moved (the share not held by the top ten named resources stayed around 48–52 in 100 in both windows), so the change is a substitution of which resources do the work, not a shift towards fewer or more resources. ### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the possibility that the allocation of work to resources changed across the offer-handling activities: A13 / A14, A17, A01, A03, A10 and A22 were executed at similar rates in both windows, but the resources performing them changed — the set dominating the earlier window (individual shares of roughly 4–11 in 100, e.g., User_46) largely gave way to a different set in the later window (e.g., User_39, whose share rose in all seven activities), and A10 in particular spread from a small group led by User_117 (about 86 in 100 events) to a much flatter allocation with over half of events outside the top ten resources. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007]

This rests on direct and consistent resource evidence: seven allocation items qualified from 14 tested resource units, against roughly 0.007 items expected by chance for that family — a count far beyond what an unchanged process produces — and all seven point in the same direction, while no control-flow item qualified and activity participation is essentially unchanged, making both a chance explanation and a structural explanation unlikely. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007]

The evidence shows who performed each activity, not how the allocation came about; it cannot separate a genuine reallocation of the same work from a difference in which resources were active in each window, and case attributes that could point to a routing or case-mix explanation are not part of the evidence. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN003]

Other differences: The single time item suggests that in the later window the elapsed time between consecutive completions from A09 to A04 is slightly longer and far more variable (median about 1.0 to about 1.2 minutes; standard deviation from about 0.3 to about 7 minutes; 132 vs 105 intervals). Because the time family produced one item against roughly 0.015 expected without change, this is within what an unchanged process typically produces at this screening strictness and does not by itself support a timing hypothesis.
