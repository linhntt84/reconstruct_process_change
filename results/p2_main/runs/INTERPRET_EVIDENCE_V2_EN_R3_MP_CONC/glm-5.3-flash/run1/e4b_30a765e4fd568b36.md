### Observed differences

- In the later window, work on four activities — A03, A13, A14 and A22 — is concentrated on fewer resources. Previously, a long tail of occasional resources carried about 56 in 100 assignments of A03, about 54 in 100 of A13 and A14, and about 45 in 100 of A22; in the later window these "other" shares fell to about 48, 48 and 28 in 100. A few resources that previously had little or no share gained most of this work: User_67 became the largest resource for A13/A14 (about 9 in 100 assignments, from essentially zero) and second for A22 (about 11 in 100), and User_79 rose to about 13 in 100 for A22 (from about 5 in 100) and 6–7 in 100 for the other activities. - The shift is substantial on every affected activity: cosine dissimilarity 0.29 for A03, 0.28 for A13 and A14, and 0.50 for A22, each larger than in about 1999 of 2000 random re-splits of unchanged data. The profiles rest on roughly 200 vs 198 assignments (A03), 277 vs 270 (A13/A14) and 119 vs 100 (A22) in the earlier vs later window. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004]
- A13 and A14 show identical before and after profiles and identical support counts, so these two items most likely capture one underlying behaviour rather than two independent changes. [ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports a change in resource allocation as a possibility: in the later window, the execution of A03, A13, A14 and A22 was reallocated from a diffuse pool of many occasionally used resources to a more concentrated set, with work shifting to resources such as User_67 and User_79 that previously held little or none of it, while resources such as User_65 fell to negligible shares. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004]

This clears the chance bar at the family level: the resource family produced 4 qualifying items against about 0.005 expected without change — a count many times the chance expectation, which random re-splitting cannot explain as a whole — and each item's difference was larger than in about 1999 of 2000 reference re-splits, with roughly 100–280 assignments per window behind each profile, making a rare-variant explanation unlikely. The control-flow family produced 0 items against about 0.17 expected, consistent with an allocation change that has no structural counterpart. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, TWAIT001]

Limitations: A13 and A14 have identical profiles and support, so they likely represent one underlying behaviour, leaving three effectively independent activities — still far above the family's chance expectation. The evidence cannot rule out a shift in case mix (different kinds of cases in the later window) as the source of the changed shares, since no case attributes are available; the anonymous resource codes also allow no statement about roles or about whether the gaining resources acted together. [ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003]

Other differences: for the A22–A03 pair, completion gaps were about 1 in 6 fewer in the later window, with the median unchanged, the mean about 30% higher and the spread roughly three times larger. The time family produced 1 item against about 0.017 expected without change — the same order as the chance expectation — so the evidence does not establish a timing change, and this pattern cannot be separated from chance sampling.
