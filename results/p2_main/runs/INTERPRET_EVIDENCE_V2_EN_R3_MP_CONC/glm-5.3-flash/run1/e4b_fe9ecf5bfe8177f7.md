### Observed differences

- Resource allocation shifted on four activities. In the earlier window, assignments of A01, A03, A17 and A22 were spread thinly: the most used single resource held only about 7–11 in 100 assignments of each activity, and a long tail of occasional resources (pooled as OTHER) held roughly 42–55 in 100. In the later window, a small group of resources — User_40, User_3 and User_49 among them — held the largest shares on all four activities (about 6–13 in 100 each; on A22 User_40 alone reached about 13 in 100), several resources that had each held roughly 3–7 in 100 dropped out of the top shares, and the OTHER tail lost about 10 percentage points on every activity (A22: about 42 in 100 down to about 32 in 100). The ten most used resources together covered about 45–58 in 100 assignments before and about 55–68 in 100 after; each of the four profiles differs more than in about 1999 of 2000 random re-splits of unchanged data. - The volume of work behind these profiles barely moved: about 200 assignments of A01 and of A03 in each window, about 271 vs 256 for A17, and about 132 vs 127 for A22 — the shift concerns who performed the activities, not how often they occurred. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004]
- No control-flow indicator passed the screening thresholds (0 items against about 0.18 expected by chance), so no change in routing, ordering or participation qualified. [screening]
- The elapsed time between consecutive completions of A16 and A24 shortened: in the earlier window the median interval was about 93 hours (mean about 76, middle half about 30–119 hours, 48 intervals); in the later window the median was about 45 hours (mean about 44, middle half about 24–52 hours, 57 intervals). ### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

A reallocation of work is supported: in the later window the execution of A01, A03, A17 and A22 concentrated on a smaller set of resources, with User_40, User_3 and User_49 taking the largest shares on all four activities, while resources that had each handled roughly 3–7 in 100 assignments of these activities in the earlier window no longer hold top shares and the tail of occasional resources shrank by about 10 percentage points per activity. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004]

Supporting signs: the four allocation profiles are direct measures of allocation and all moved in the same direction with matching gaining and losing groups; the resource family produced 4 qualifying items against about 0.005 expected without a change — hundreds of times the chance level — so the pattern as a whole is unlikely to be a sampling artefact; assignment volumes were stable; and no control-flow item qualified, which argues against the shift being a by-product of activities occurring at different rates or of paths being replaced. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, screening]

Limitations and competing explanation: the four profiles may express one underlying reallocation rather than four independent changes; the evidence shows which shares moved but not whether the gaining resources took over the losing resources' work or the latter stopped appearing for other reasons, and it cannot rule out small residual shares for the losing resources below the reported top ten. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004]

Other differences: the elapsed time between consecutive completions of A16 and A24 fell to roughly half (median about 93 to about 45 hours; mean about 76 to about 44 hours), with somewhat more intervals observed (48 vs 57). As the only qualifying time item (1 against about 0.019 expected without change), it would not by itself establish a timing change, and the evidence cannot link it to, or separate it from, the reallocation. [TWAIT001, screening]
