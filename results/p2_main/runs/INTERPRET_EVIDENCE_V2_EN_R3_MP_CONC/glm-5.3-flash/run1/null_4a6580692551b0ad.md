### Observed differences

- The typical order of A35 and A36 between A31 and A32 reversed. In the earlier window the dominant pattern was consistent with the chain A31 → A36 → A35 → A32: A31 was immediately followed by A36 in about 60 in 100 qualifying traces, A36 immediately followed by A35 in essentially all observed cases, and A35 immediately followed by A32 in about 58 in 100. In the later window the dominant pattern was A31 → A35 → A36 → A32: A31 was immediately followed by A35 in about 61 in 100, while A31→A36 and A35→A32 were now typically two steps apart (about 61 and 62 in 100) because the other activity sits between them. [LAG001, LAG002, LAG003, LAG004]
- Successor shares moved accordingly. From A35, the share of transitions to A36 rose from about 38% to about 61%, while transitions to A32 fell from about 58% to about 37%; from A36, transitions to A32 rose from about 39% to about 60%, while transitions back to A35 fell from about 60% to about 36%. [DIR001, DIR002]
- The direct step A36 → A35 still exists in the later window but is taken less often: traces in which A36 precedes A35 within five steps fell from 62 to 34 out of 200 per window, and when the step occurs it remains almost always immediately adjacent (about 94 in 100 in the later window). [LAG004, DIR002]
- Both orderings were present in both windows and only their shares changed; each profile rests on roughly 87–104 traces per window (about 44–52 in 100 traces contain the activities involved), so none of the activities involved is rare. [DIR001, DIR002, LAG001, LAG002, LAG003, LAG004]
- In the later window a few rare outgoing transitions appear that are absent from the earlier profiles, each at about 1 in 100 transitions: from A35 to A25 and A34, and from A36 to A21, A30, A25 and A07. [DIR001, DIR002]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the hypothesis that the two existing orderings of A35 and A36 between A31 and A32 exchanged frequencies: in the earlier window A31 → A36 → A35 → A32 was the majority pattern (about 60 in 100, versus about 38–40 in 100 for A31 → A35 → A36), and in the later window A31 → A35 → A36 → A32 became the majority (about 61 in 100, versus about 36–38 in 100). Because both orderings and all four activities were observed in substantial shares in both windows, and only the shares between them shifted, this is a change in how often existing paths are taken rather than the addition, removal or bypassing of a path. [LAG001, LAG002, LAG003, DIR001, DIR002]

Supporting signs: six control-flow items qualified where about 0.5 would be expected without a change (twelve times the chance expectation), and each difference was larger than in about 995 of 1,000 random re-splits of the pooled traces. The successor-share shifts and the position-gap shifts show the same reversal directly and consistently; the six items all concern the same four activities and most plausibly reflect one underlying difference rather than six independent changes. [DIR001, DIR002, LAG001, LAG002, LAG003, LAG004]

Limitations: the indicators measure event positions, not clock time, so no conclusion about timing is possible; and the evidence cannot distinguish a changed ordering constraint from a shift in how the choice between the two orderings is made upstream (for example at A31), since both fit the data equally. The A36→A35 profile rests on only 34 later traces, so that item is weak on its own. [LAG004, DIR002]

Other differences: only the rare new outgoing transitions noted above (about 1 in 100 each, from A35 to A25/A34 and from A36 to A21/A30/A25/A07), which at this size may be sampling noise rather than a behavioural change. [DIR001, DIR002]
