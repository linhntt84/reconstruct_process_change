### Observed differences

- Work on six activities (A01, A03, A13, A14, A17, A21) was reallocated between resources. In the earlier window, each of these activities was shared among a stable group of roughly ten resources, each holding about 3–10 in 100 of that activity's assignments (for example, User_3 held about 7 in 100 of A01). In the later window, most of that group no longer appears among the main assignees, while a different group of similar size appears, led by User_78 with about 8–12 in 100 of every one of these activities — the largest named share in each case. A few resources, such as User_61, kept a similar share (about 5–8 in 100) in both windows. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]
- The redistribution is consistent in direction across all six activities — the same resources lose share and the same new ones gain — and allocation also became somewhat more dispersed: resources outside the top ten together rose from roughly 41–53 in 100 of assignments to roughly 48–56 in 100. Each of these differences is larger than in about 999.5 of 1,000 random re-splits of the pooled data. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]
- A13 and A14 show identical allocation profiles in both windows, so these two items likely describe the same underlying assignments rather than two independent changes. [ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004]
- The elapsed time between consecutive completion events roughly doubled: the mean rose from about 29 to about 56 hours and the median from about 22 to about 45 hours, with the upper quartile rising from about 33 to about 72 hours while the lower quartile stayed near zero. [TWAIT001]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the possibility that resource allocation changed for activities A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A21: in the earlier window a group of about ten resources, including User_3, each handled roughly 3–10 in 100 of these activities' assignments, while in the later window that group has largely disappeared from the top assignees and a different group led by User_78 (about 8–12 in 100 each) has taken over, with resources such as User_61 continuing at a similar share. Supporting signs: all six activity-level allocation profiles shift in the same direction with the same resources entering and leaving; the resource family produced six qualifying items where about 0.005 were expected without change, many times its chance expectation (control flow produced none against about 0.17 expected, time one against about 0.02); and each profile rests on roughly 130–300 assignment events per window. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, TWAIT001]

Limitations: the evidence shows which resources gained and lost share but not why; it cannot distinguish a reallocation specific to these six activities from a broader change in which resources are active across the whole log, and because A13 and A14 have identical profiles the six items are not fully independent confirmations. [ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004]

Other differences: the elapsed time between consecutive completion events roughly doubled (median about 22 hours before, about 45 hours after), but the time family yielded a single qualifying item where about 0.02 were expected without change — within what an unchanged process typically produces — so no timing change is inferred, and any link between this and the reallocation would be speculation. [TWAIT001]
