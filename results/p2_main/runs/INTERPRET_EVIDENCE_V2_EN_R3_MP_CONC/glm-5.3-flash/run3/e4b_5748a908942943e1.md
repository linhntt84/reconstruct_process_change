### Observed differences

- In the later window, work on six activities — A01, A03, A13, A14, A16 and A17 — is assigned to a largely different set of resources than before. Earlier, each of these activities was spread thinly: for A01, the ten most-used resources each handled roughly 3–8 in 100 assignments and about half of the work went to a long tail of others; in the later window, a mostly new set of resources holds the top shares (User_49 now leads A01, A03 and A17 with about 8 in 100), while resources that previously each handled about 4–7 in 100 no longer appear among the top shares. The same resources gain and the same resources lose across all six activities [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN006].
- A16 changed from widely shared to single-handed: earlier its work was spread over 18 distinct resources with the top resource (User_123) taking about 14 in 100 assignments; in the later window User_123 took every one of about 165 A16 observations (vs 154 earlier), and the concentration index rose from about 0.10 to 1.0 [ASSIGN005, CONC001].

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the hypothesis of a change in resource allocation: the work on activities A01, A03, A13, A14, A16 and A17, which in the earlier window was spread across a broad pool of resources (top shares of roughly 3–8 in 100 each, with about half in a long tail), was in the later window taken over by a largely different set of resources, with A16 as the extreme case, going from about 18 distinct resources to a single resource (User_123) handling all of its assignments. Supporting signs: the resource family yielded 7 qualifying items against a chance expectation of about 0.006, an excess that random re-splits of unchanged data cannot produce as a whole; the shift is consistent rather than scattered, with the same resources gaining and losing across all six activities; and the concentration item shows the A16 change directly. No control-flow item qualified, so the detectable change is confined to allocation rather than structure [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, CONC001].

Limitations: the resource codes are anonymous, so the evidence shows that the allocation shifted but not why; a shift in the case mix or in which resources were active could produce the same redistribution without any change in assignment rules, and the evidence cannot rule this out. Also, A13 and A14 show identical profiles and identical support counts, so several items likely mirror one underlying shift rather than independent confirmations [ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004].

Other differences: The elapsed time between consecutive completions of A04 and A01 fell consistently (mean about 48 to about 29 hours; median about 30 to about 22; upper quartile about 80 to about 47). This rests on a single time item, against a family-wide chance expectation of about 0.013, so it suggests a possible timing change that this evidence alone cannot establish [TWAIT001].
