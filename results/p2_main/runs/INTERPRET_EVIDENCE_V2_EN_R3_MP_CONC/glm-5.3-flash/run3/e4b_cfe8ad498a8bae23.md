### Observed differences

- The resources executing a set of activities changed almost completely. In the earlier window, assignments for A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A22 were spread over a broad pool in which one resource, User_105, held the largest single share (about 10–16% per activity) alongside a long tail of occasional resources (roughly 30–45% combined). In the later window, User_105 no longer appears among the top shares of any of these activities, and a largely new group of resources — led by User_72 at roughly 7–13% — now carries the work, with largely the same resources gaining and losing across all six activities. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007]
- Activity A16 shows the extreme case: earlier its work was divided across about 20 distinct resources, with the busiest handling about 21% of assignments; later a single resource, User_17, performed all of them (concentration index about 0.09 versus 1.0). [ASSIGN005, CONC001]
- The volume of work behind these activities barely moved — for example 200 versus 200 observations for A01 and 135 versus 136 for A16 — so the difference lies in who performs the work, not in how often the activities run. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN005]
- Elapsed time between consecutive completions involving A07 and A13 fell sharply: a median of about 20 hours in the earlier window versus about 1.7 hours in the later one (means about 16.5 versus 3.5 hours), and such consecutive pairs were observed about three times as often (6 versus 17 observations); this rests on few observations. [TWAIT001]
- Screening found no qualifying control-flow difference (0 items against about 0.17 expected by chance); all qualifying items concern time and resources. [TWAIT001, ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007, CONC001]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the possibility that resource allocation changed: the activities A01, A03, A13, A14, A16, A17 and A22 were reassigned from one group of resources, which included User_105, to a largely different group, which includes User_72, with A16 becoming the exclusive responsibility of User_17. The change is measured directly by the assignment and concentration indicators, shows the same gainers and losers across six activities, and is far beyond what chance would produce — eight resource items were observed where about 0.005 would be expected for the ten resource units tested, and every item's difference exceeds about 999.5 in 1000 random re-splits. Because activity volumes are essentially unchanged, the pattern fits a reallocation of the same work rather than a change in how often activities run. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007, CONC001]

Limitations: the evidence shows that the work moved but not why. It cannot rule out that the two windows simply cover periods in which different resources were available, and for A16 in particular a change in how its events were recorded (attributions collapsing onto a single code) would look identical. The seven activities also include two with identical profiles (A13, A14), so the agreeing items are not fully independent confirmations. [ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, CONC001]

Other differences: A timing change is suggested separately — the elapsed time between consecutive completions of A07 and A13 dropped from a median of about 20 hours to about 1.7 hours while the pair occurred about three times as often (6 versus 17 observations). This is a single time item (against about 0.014 expected by chance) based on few observations, so it indicates a possible change in timing without establishing one, and the evidence cannot show whether it is connected to the reallocation. [TWAIT001]
