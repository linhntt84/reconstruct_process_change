### Observed differences

- In the later window, the elapsed time between consecutive completions of A25 and A32 roughly halved: in the earlier window these gaps averaged about 0.86 hours (median about 0.89, middle half about 0.77–0.96 hours), while in the later window they averaged about 0.42 hours (median about 0.43, middle half about 0.40–0.46 hours). The spread also narrowed (standard deviation about 0.14 vs 0.05 hours), and a similar number of gaps was observed in each window (about 39 before, 31 after). [TWAIT001]
- The elapsed time between consecutive completions of A34 and A32 also roughly halved: from about 0.13 hours on average in the earlier window (median about 0.12) to about 0.06 hours in the later window (median about 0.04), again with a similar number of observations (about 32 before, 33 after). [TWAIT002]
- Both timing differences are unusually large for random re-splits of the pooled data: only about 1 in 2000 random re-splits produced a difference this size, and since the frequency of both activity pairs stayed broadly stable, the change lies in the gap durations rather than in how often the pairs occur. [TWAIT001, TWAIT002]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the possibility of a timing change around A32: in the later window, the elapsed time from the completion of the preceding activity to the completion of A32 was much shorter than before, both after A25 (about 0.86 down to about 0.42 hours on average) and after A34 (about 0.13 down to about 0.06 hours). Supporting signs: both gaps decreased consistently across mean, median and quartiles; both differences exceed all but about 1 in 2000 random re-splits; the two qualifying time items far exceed the roughly 0.01 expected by chance among the 25 time units tested, so chance alone cannot account for them; and the control-flow family produced no items (0 of 59 units, against about 0.29 expected without change), consistent with a timing change that needs no structural counterpart. [TWAIT001, TWAIT002]

Limitations: TWAIT measures elapsed time between consecutive completion events, so the evidence shows the gaps shortened but not what caused this; both items share A32 and may reflect one underlying difference rather than two independent ones; and the samples are finite (about 31–39 gaps per window). An important competing explanation the evidence cannot rule out is a shift in which events count as consecutive — if different activities now run between these pairs, the composition of the measured gaps would change as well as their length — although the broadly stable gap counts make a large compositional shift less likely. [TWAIT001, TWAIT002]
