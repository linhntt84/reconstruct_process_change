### Observed differences

- Resource allocation changed across most monitored activities. For A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A22, assignments moved from one group of resources to a largely different one: in the earlier window the same resources recur as top assignees (User_105 handled about 10–16 in 100 assignments of each of these activities), while in the later window User_72 is among the most frequent (about 7–13 in 100) and User_105 no longer appears among the top shares. The overall spread stayed similar, with about 30–45 in 100 of assignments in an unlabelled remainder in both windows, and each activity appeared at a similar rate in both windows. Allocation-profile dissimilarity is about 0.35–0.49 on a 0–1 scale, and each difference is larger than in about 999.5 of 1,000 random re-splits. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007]
- A16 changed most sharply: earlier it was shared across about 20 distinct resources, with the busiest handling about 21 in 100 occurrences; in the later window a single resource, User_17, performed all of them (top share 100 in 100, concentration index from about 0.09 to 1.0). A16 itself occurred at a similar rate in both windows (about 68 in 100 traces), so participation did not change — only who did the work. [ASSIGN005, CONC001]
- The elapsed time between consecutive completions of A07 and A13 fell from a mean of about 16.5 hours (median about 20 hours) to about 3.5 hours (median about 1.7 hours), with the spread narrowing accordingly, and the number of such consecutive completion pairs rose from 6 to 17. This rests on few observations (6 before, 17 after). [TWAIT001]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the possibility of a change in resource allocation: the execution of A01, A03, A13, A14, A16, A17 and A22 was handed over from one group of resources to a largely different group, with A16 consolidated from about twenty resources onto a single resource (User_17). The screening comparison makes chance an unlikely explanation: the resources family produced 8 evidence items where about 0.005 would be expected without a change, while control flow produced none against about 0.17 and timing one against about 0.014; every resource item's difference also exceeds about 999.5 of 1,000 random re-splits, the direction is the same across all seven activities, and the profiles rest on substantial support (about 200 observations per window for A01 and A03, roughly 135–260 for the others). [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007, CONC001]

Limitations: the evidence shows which resources performed the activities, not why the work moved; about 40 in 100 assignments sit in an unlabelled remainder in both windows, so part of the shift is not itemised; and A13 and A14 show identical profiles, so they likely reflect one shared shift rather than two independent changes. An important competing explanation the evidence cannot rule out is a change in the pool of active resources (some resources replaced by others) rather than a reallocation within a stable pool — both would produce the same pattern. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007]

Other differences: the elapsed time between consecutive completions of A07 and A13 dropped by about 13 hours on average (mean about 16.5 to about 3.5 hours) — a timing change that could stand as a hypothesis in its own right, but it rests on only 6 before and 17 after observations, and since A13 is also among the reallocated activities, the evidence cannot show whether the shorter gaps and the handover are linked.
