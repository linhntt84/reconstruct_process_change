### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the hypothesis that resource allocation changed for A01, A03, A13, A14 and A22: in the later window, assignments were redistributed from the group of previously active resources to a new group of resources, with User_67 remaining the most used single resource and increasing its share, User_56 and other newcomers taking over the mid-sized shares, and the previous mid-sized resources dropping to zero or near-zero. The pattern is direct (the assignment profiles measure allocation itself), consistent (the same newcomer group gains and the same previous group loses on every one of the five activities) and rests on about 130–275 assignment observations per activity and window. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005]

The screening comparison supports this reading: the resource family produced 5 qualifying items where roughly 0.005 were expected without any change — far beyond what a stable process typically yields — while the time family produced 1 item against roughly 0.016 expected and the control-flow family 0 items against roughly 0.18 expected, so no structural or routing difference passed the thresholds. [TWAIT001, ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005]

Limitation: the profiles are aggregate shares, so the evidence shows that allocation moved but not how it was organised; an important competing explanation it cannot rule out is that a changed mix of cases between the windows shifted the resource profiles indirectly rather than through a reassignment of these activities. The resource codes are anonymous, so no roles or teams can be inferred. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005]

Other differences: the elapsed time between consecutive completion events of A09 and A23 rose from a typical (median) value of about 1 hour to about 14.3 hours. This is a timing difference in its own right, but it rests on a single qualifying item (against roughly 0.016 expected by chance in the time family) and on modest counts (29 vs 25 observed gaps), and the evidence cannot show whether it is connected to the resource reallocation.
