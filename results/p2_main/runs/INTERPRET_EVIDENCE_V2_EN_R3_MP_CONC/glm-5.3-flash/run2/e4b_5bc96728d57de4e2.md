### Observed differences

- Work on five activities (A01, A03, A13, A14, A22) was reallocated between resources. In the earlier window, the largest shares of each activity were handled by a recurring group of resources, most visibly User_56 (roughly 9–20% of an activity's assignments) and User_78 (roughly 7–16%). In the later window, both lost share on every one of these five activities — on A22, User_56 fell from about 20% to about 12% and User_78 from about 16% to below the ten most used — while a largely different group gained, most visibly User_42, which rose on all five activities (on A22 from about 4% to about 9%). On A22, work also spread more widely: the share handled by resources outside the ten most used rose from about 20% to about 33%. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005]
- The elapsed time between the completions of A04 and A01, observed in essentially all 200 traces in each window, fell sharply but became more uneven: the median gap dropped from about 68 hours to about 21 hours and the mean from about 57 to about 29 hours, while the spread grew (standard deviation from about 47 to about 76 hours). - The elapsed time between completions of A09 and A23 also dropped, from a median of about 13 hours to about 1 hour; this rests on a moderate base of 35 observed pairs before and 25 after. [TWAIT004]
- Two further flagged pairs (A02→A12, A05→A09) concern gaps of a few hundredths of a second in both windows; the differences are statistically unusual but behaviourally negligible. [TWAIT001, TWAIT003]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports a change in resource allocation: in the later window, assignments on A01, A03, A13, A14 and A22 moved away from the group of resources that previously held the largest shares (most visibly User_56 and User_78) toward a largely different group (most visibly User_42), with A22's work additionally spread across more resources overall. The resource family produced 5 flagged items where roughly 0.005 would be expected if nothing had changed — far more than chance — each difference exceeds those of all but about 1 in 2,000 random re-splits of the pooled data, the indicator measures allocation directly, and the direction is consistent, with the same resources losing and the same others gaining across all five activities. Limitations: A13 and A14 show identical profiles, so the items describe at most four distinct activity behaviours and are not independent confirmations; and the evidence shows which resources' shares changed but cannot distinguish a change in assignment behaviour from a change in which resources were active in each window.

Other differences: timing changed as well — the A04→A01 completion gap fell to roughly a third of its earlier typical value while becoming more variable, and the A09→A23 gap fell from a median of about 13 hours to about 1 hour (based on only 35 and 25 observations). These are direct timing findings in their own right; no structural counterpart appears in the evidence, since no control-flow item qualified. An important competing explanation remains: the timing shifts and the reallocation could be two faces of one underlying reorganisation, or one a by-product of the other, and the evidence cannot link or order them.
