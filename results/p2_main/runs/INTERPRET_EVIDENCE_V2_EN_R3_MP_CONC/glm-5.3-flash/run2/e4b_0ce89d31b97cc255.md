### Observed differences

- For six activities — A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A22 — the allocation of work across resources changed substantially. In the earlier window, each activity's events were led by a consistent group of resources, each handling roughly 4–11 in 100 events (for A01, User_37 about 10 in 100). In the later window, most of these resources' shares fell to a few percent or dropped out of the leading shares, while a largely new group took over at comparable shares (for A01, User_3 rose from no share to about 9 in 100). - The shift is consistent in direction across all six activities: the same previously prominent resources recede everywhere and the same new resources appear everywhere, while a few resources continued and gained share (User_42 went from about 5 to about 10 in 100 for A03). The diffuse remainder of many minor resources stayed at roughly 38–48 in 100 in both windows. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]
- The size of the reallocation is similar across activities (about 0.42–0.45 on the assignment-share dissimilarity scale), and each difference is larger than about 1,995 of 2,000 random re-splits of the pooled windows. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]
- Participation in the activities did not change: A01 appears in essentially every trace in both windows (200 of 200), A22 in about 65 in 100 traces in both (131 of 200 before, 137 of 200 after), and the other activities show similar support in both windows. The difference lies in which resources perform the activities, not in how often the activities run. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the hypothesis that, in the later window, the work on A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A22 was reallocated from one group of resources to a largely different group: resources that previously took the largest shares (for example User_37, about 8–11 in 100 of each activity's events) now handle little or none, resources previously not prominent (for example User_3, about 8–9 in 100) took over, and a few resources such as User_42 continued at similar or higher shares. Supporting signs: the change is measured directly on the assignment shares of each activity, it points the same way for all six activities, each difference exceeds about 1,995 of 2,000 random re-splits, participation levels are unchanged so the shift is in allocation rather than activity frequency, and six qualifying resource items against roughly 0.004 expected without change is far beyond what an unchanged process produces. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]

Limitations: all six items come from a single indicator family (assignment shares), with no corroborating handover or concentration evidence, and A13 and A14 show identical profiles, so the items are not fully independent. A competing explanation the evidence cannot rule out is that the mix of cases changed, with different case types being handled by different resources, rather than a reallocation of like-for-like work.
