### Observed differences

- In the earlier window, A25, A26, A31 and A32 each occurred exactly once in every trace (participation 100 in 100, mean 1.0 occurrence per trace, no within-trace repeats). In the later window they still appear in every trace, but average about 3.1 occurrences per trace and repeat within the same trace in about 69 in 100 cases. [OCC001, OCC002, OCC003, OCC004]
- The flow out of A32 changed: before, every observed outgoing transition led to A20; in the later window about 68 in 100 lead to A25 and about 32 in 100 still lead to A20 (based on 620 outgoing transitions in the later window versus 200 before). [DIR001]
- The fixed sequencing among these four activities largely broke down. Before, A25 preceded A26, A31 and A32 in all traces, A32 followed both A26 and A31, and only A26/A31 lacked a fixed order (about 52 versus 48 in 100). In the later window, about 69 in 100 traces show these pairs interleaved or repeated, while the original strict order survives in about 31 in 100. [REL001, REL002, REL003, REL004, REL005, REL006]
- Distances between the activities stretched: pairs that were always 1–2 steps apart before now sit at 1–2 steps in only about a fifth of instances or fewer, and gaps of 3–5 steps appear; the ordered pair A26→A31 within five steps occurs in 173 of 200 later traces versus 104 of 200 before. [LAG001, LAG002, LAG003, LAG004, LAG005, LAG006]
- The position spread of each of the four activities roughly doubled (standard deviation from about 0.06 to about 0.13 of the trace) with only slightly later medians, consistent with several occurrences scattered through the trace rather than a single activity being moved. [POS001, POS002, POS003, POS004]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the possibility that a repetition loop now covers A25, A26, A31 and A32: four activities that previously each ran exactly once per trace in a largely fixed sequence (A25 first, A32 last, A26 and A31 in either order) are executed about three times per trace in roughly two thirds of later cases, and A32 now typically flows back to A25 (about 68 in 100 of its outgoing transitions) instead of proceeding to A20. [OCC001, OCC002, OCC003, OCC004, DIR001, REL001, REL002, REL003, REL004, REL005, REL006]

Supporting signs: nineteen items qualified where an unchanged process would typically produce fewer than one, and every item's difference is larger than in about 199 of 200 random re-splits of the pooled data; the four occurrence profiles changed identically and simultaneously while participation stayed at 100 in 100, the signature of a block being re-executed rather than of activities appearing or disappearing; the successor redirection at A32 is observed directly; and the share of traces with interleaved or repeated pairs (about 69 in 100) closely matches the share of A32→A25 transitions (about 68 in 100), with the surviving strict order (about 31 in 100) matching the residual A32→A20 share; the longer lags and doubled position spread fit repeated blocks rather than a moved activity. [OCC001, OCC002, OCC003, OCC004, DIR001, REL001, REL002, REL003, REL004, REL005, REL006, LAG001, LAG002, LAG003, LAG004, LAG005, LAG006, POS001, POS002, POS003, POS004]

Limitations: only the A32→A25 redirection is observed directly; the evidence contains no outgoing-transition profile for A25, A26 or A31, so the exact shape of the loop (whether all four activities re-execute in every round, and where the loop is exited) cannot be confirmed. A competing explanation the evidence cannot rule out is that the repetition is driven by a loop entering before A25, with the A32→A25 change a consequence rather than the driver; the numerical agreement above makes this less likely but does not exclude it.
