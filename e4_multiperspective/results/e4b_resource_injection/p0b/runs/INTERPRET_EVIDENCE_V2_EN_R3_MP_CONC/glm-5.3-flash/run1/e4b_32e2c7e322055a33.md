### Observed differences

- Work on many activities was handled by different resources in the later window. In the earlier window, a recurring set of resources dominated assignments for A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A22 — for example User_3 was the largest assignee on A01 (about 10 in 100 assignments) and on A22 (about 13 in 100), and User_8 led A13, A14 and A17 (about 11–12 in 100). In the later window these resources no longer appear among the main assignees, a largely different set took their place (User_49 is the largest on A22 at about 12 in 100), and the share falling to many smaller resources outside the top ten rose on every one of these activities — for example from about 30 to about 35 in 100 on A22 and from about 39 to about 48 in 100 on A13/A14. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007]

- A10 shows the starkest shift in concentration: in the earlier window a small group of resources carried about 83 in 100 A10 executions (largest single share about 16 in 100, with only about 9 in 100 left for everyone else), while in the later window no resource exceeded about 9 in 100 and about 47 in 100 of executions went to a long tail of other resources. [ASSIGN003]

- A13 and A14 have identical allocation profiles and identical change values, so they most likely reflect one underlying behaviour rather than two independent changes. [ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005]

- The observed outgoing transitions of A15 were redistributed: earlier, about half of some 22 observed transitions went to A24 and about 45 in 100 looped back to A15 itself (a small share to A21); in the later window, about 87 in 100 of some 23 transitions went to A24, about 9 in 100 looped back, a small share went to A22, and A21 was no longer observed. This rests on only about two dozen observed transitions per window. [DIR001]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the possibility that the allocation of work to resources changed: in the earlier window, executions of A01, A03, A10, A13, A14, A17 and A22 were concentrated on a recurring group of resources (User_3 and User_8 among the most used, plus a dedicated group for A10), while in the later window these resources lost their share to a largely different set (for example User_49) and to a much larger long tail, so that handling of all these activities became more dispersed. The hypothesis is direct (assignment profiles measure allocation itself), consistent (all seven resource items show the same pattern of previously dominant codes declining and new codes rising, each difference larger than in at least 999 of 1000 random re-splits), and difficult to explain by chance: 7 resource items qualified where roughly 1 in 200 would be expected if nothing had changed. The observation volumes behind each profile were similar in both windows (for example about 200 for A01 in each window and about 300 for A13), so the shift is not an artefact of the activities appearing or disappearing. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007]

Limitations: the resource codes are anonymous, so the evidence shows a reallocation between groups of resources but cannot distinguish work being actively reassigned within a stable resource population from a different set of resources simply being active in the later window; and because A13 and A14 show identical profiles, their items are not independent confirmations. [ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005]

Other differences: the routing out of A15 shifted towards A24 with the self-loop share collapsing, as described above. This is the only control-flow item (1 against about 0.18 expected without change) and it rests on about 22–23 observed transitions per window, so sampling fluctuation cannot be ruled out; it is therefore not added to the main hypothesis. [DIR001]
