### Observed differences

- Work on activities A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A21 moved between groups of resources. In the earlier window, assignments of each of these activities were spread widely: the most used resources, including User_49, each held roughly 5–9 in 100 assignments and none exceeded about 9 in 100. In the later window, User_67 held about 9–14 in 100 assignments of every one of these activities (about 14 in 100 for A21) and User_132 about 6–12 in 100, while the previously frequent group, including User_49, fell to about 4 in 100 or less, with several of its members no longer among the ten most used. The residual pool of remaining resources stayed similar at roughly 37–50 in 100 in both windows. Each profile difference scored 0.35–0.50 on a 0–1 dissimilarity scale and exceeded about 999.5 of 1,000 random re-splits of the pooled data. - The volume of work behind these profiles was stable: assignment counts per activity changed only slightly between windows (for example 200 vs 200 for A01, 267 vs 270 for A13, 244 vs 253 for A17 and 135 vs 138 for A21), so the shift is in who performs the activities, not in how often they run. - The elapsed time between consecutive completions of A05 and A09 became heavier in its upper tail. In the earlier window the gaps were tightly concentrated (median about 1.0×10⁻⁵ hours, 75th percentile about 1.1×10⁻⁵); in the later window the median rose only about 14% (to about 1.1×10⁻⁵ hours) while the 75th percentile grew about nineteen-fold (to about 2.2×10⁻⁴ hours) and the mean about eight-fold, so the lengthening comes from the slowest gaps rather than from all of them. The number of observed gaps fell slightly (120 vs 113). [TWAIT001]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the possibility of a change in resource allocation: in the later window, the work of activities A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A21 was reallocated from a group of previously frequently used resources (including User_49) to a new group led by User_67 and User_132, with User_67 becoming the most used resource for all six activities while participation in the activities themselves stayed stable. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]

The screening comparison supports this reading: per family, control flow produced 0 qualifying items against about 0.16 expected by chance, time produced 1 against about 0.015, and resources produced 6 against about 0.005 — only the resource count is many times its chance expectation, so the resource pattern as a whole cannot be a sampling artefact. Consistency points the same way: all six profiles show the same resources rising and the same resources falling, and each difference exceeded about 999.5 of 1,000 random re-splits. [TWAIT001, ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]

Limitations: the profiles decompose only the ten most used resources plus a residual pool of roughly 37–50 in 100 assignments, so shifts inside that pool are invisible, and resources near the top-ten boundary can drop in or out between windows by chance; the profiles for A13 and A14 are identical, so the six items are not fully independent. An important competing explanation the evidence cannot rule out is that part of the measured shift reflects such boundary churn rather than a coordinated reallocation, although the same-direction movement across all six activities makes pure chance unlikely. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006]

Other differences: the tail-lengthening of the elapsed times between consecutive completions of A05 and A09 described above. As the only qualifying time item, against a chance expectation of about 0.015 items, it could also be a sampling artefact; if real, it would constitute a timing change in its own right, but the evidence cannot confirm it or tie it to the reallocation.
