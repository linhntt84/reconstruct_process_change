### Observed differences

- Work allocation changed for most screened activities. In the earlier window, A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A22 were each dominated by a stable group of resource codes, led by codes handling about 10–13 assignments in 100 (for example User_3 with about 10 in 100 of A01 assignments and about 13 in 100 of A22 assignments, and User_8 with about 11–12 in 100 on A13, A14 and A17). In the later window these codes had lost nearly all of that share, and a largely different group took their place (for example User_5, at about 8 assignments in 100 on A13, A14 and A17 after being absent from their top codes before) [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007].
- Allocation also became less concentrated. The share of assignments going to codes outside the ten most frequent ones rose for every affected activity: most sharply for A10, where a dedicated group of codes with shares of about 8–16 in 100 each collapsed to at most about 9 in 100 while the outside share jumped from about 9 in 100 to about 47 in 100, and for A13, A14 and A17, where it rose from about 38–39 in 100 to about 48 in 100 [ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006].
- Each of these seven profiles rests on roughly 120–320 assignment observations per window, and every difference is larger than in all but about 1 in 2,000 random re-splits of the pooled data [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007].
- One control-flow difference appeared at A15: in the earlier window, about 50 in 100 of its observed outgoing transitions went to A24 and about 45 in 100 returned to A15 itself; in the later window, about 87 in 100 went to A24 and only about 9 in 100 returned to A15, with a small share to A22 where A21 had previously appeared. This rests on only about 22–23 observed transitions [DIR001].

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports one specific possibility: a change in resource allocation, in which assignments for A01, A03, A10, A13, A14, A17 and A22 shifted from a group of previously dominant resource codes to a largely different group of codes, with allocation becoming less concentrated overall. The signs are direct (the items measure assignment shares themselves), consistent (all seven items show the same direction, with the same old codes losing and the same new codes gaining across activities), and far beyond chance: the resource family produced 7 qualifying items where about 0.005 were expected if nothing had changed [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007].

Limitations: the evidence shows which code groups gained and lost share but not why, and it cannot rule out that the active pool of resource codes itself changed between the windows (for example, old codes replaced by new ones) rather than work being routed differently within a stable pool. A13 and A14 show identical profiles in both windows, so they reflect one underlying behaviour rather than two independent confirmations [ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005].

Other differences: A15's outgoing transition mix shifted — self-repetition fell from about 45 in 100 transitions to about 9 in 100, while transitions to A24 rose from about 50 to about 87 in 100 — but control flow produced only 1 qualifying item where about 0.18 were expected by chance, which is what an unchanged process typically yields, and the profile rests on about 22–23 transitions; this alone does not support a structural change [DIR001].
