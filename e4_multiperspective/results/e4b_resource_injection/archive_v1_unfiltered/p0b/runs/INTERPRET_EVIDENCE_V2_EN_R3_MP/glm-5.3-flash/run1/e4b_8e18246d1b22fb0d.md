### Observed differences

- On six activities — A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A22 — assignment work moved from one group of resources to a largely different group. In the earlier window a few resources held the largest shares (User_11 about 10–16% per activity, with several peers at roughly 4–15% each); in the later window User_11 and the other leading resources of that group hold no reported share, or only a few percent, while a largely new set of resources — User_56 the largest at about 6–12% per activity, plus roughly nine further resources at about 3–8% each — took the work over. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007]
- Allocation also became more dispersed: the share of assignments spread across many minor resources rose from about 36–44 in 100 to about 47–49 in 100 on five of these activities (on A22 from about 23 to 39 in 100), and on A22 the top three resources had together held about 45 in 100 assignments before, whereas afterwards no single resource exceeded about 12 in 100. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007]
- A10 moved in the opposite direction: earlier its assignments were spread over about ten resources (largest share about 17 in 100, next about 13 and 11), while in the later window a single resource, User_126, holds all reported A10 assignments. [ASSIGN003]
- How often the activities ran stayed similar — A01 appears in 200 of 200 traces in both windows and A22 in about 112 vs 109 of 200 — so these differences concern who was assigned, not whether the activities executed. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN007]

### Inference

Support level: Supported hypothesis

The evidence supports the hypothesis of a reallocation of work between two groups of resources: in the later window, A01, A03, A13, A14, A17 and A22, previously dominated by a group that included User_11 (about 10–16% each), are assigned mainly to a largely different group led by User_56 (about 6–12% each), with a larger residual share spread across minor resources. Chance is an unlikely explanation: seven resource items qualified where about 0.005 were expected without change, while control flow and timing produced no items (0 against 0.17 and 0.014 expected); all items point the same way, with the same groups rising and falling, and each difference exceeds all but about 1 in 2000 random re-splits of the pooled data. Participation in each activity was similar in both windows, so the profiles reflect who performed the work rather than how often. Limitations: only assignment shares are observed, so the evidence cannot show how the work was transferred (for example through handovers), and A13 and A14 have identical profiles, so they represent one pattern rather than two independent confirmations. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN002, ASSIGN003, ASSIGN004, ASSIGN005, ASSIGN006, ASSIGN007]

Other differences: A10's allocation concentrated onto a single resource, User_126, from about ten shared resources before — also an allocation change, but one of concentration rather than the group replacement seen on the other activities. [ASSIGN003]

Competing explanation: a shift in the mix of cases reaching the later window, with different kinds of cases routed to different resources, could produce the same reallocation signature; the evidence cannot separate a change in allocation behaviour from such a case-mix shift. [ASSIGN001, ASSIGN003]
